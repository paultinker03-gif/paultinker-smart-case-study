Case study · Smart Pension

Designing pensions people can understand

I led product design across Smart's member, adviser and employer products, and designed Keystone, the design system behind them: three audiences, four brands and two reading directions.

Smart grew from around 150 to 800 people while I was there, and the platform grew to 1.5 million users and over £15 billion under management by 2025, up from £0.21 billion in 2018. Members, advisers and employers had inconsistent experiences across Smart's brand, its white-label partners and several languages, accessibility needed work, and teams were solving the same problems independently.

The challenge

The clearest signal came from support. Members, advisers and employers regularly called to ask Smart to make changes for them, because they couldn't work out how to do it themselves. The features existed, but people either couldn't find them or, when they did, couldn't work out how to use them.

The existing platform wasn't built for Smart's growth: teams worked independently, Design and Engineering lacked a common language, and improvements made in one product rarely reached the others. The member, adviser and employer experiences were each designed and built differently, with nothing shared between them. Every new feature or change had to be designed and built separately for each one, so it was slow to ship. The symptoms looked like UI problems; the underlying challenge was building a shared platform that could scale with the organisation.

Building the Design System

I approached the Design System as a product, not a component library, working hands-on across tokens, components, patterns, templates, accessibility, responsive behaviour and documentation, with Design and Engineering deliberately brought together around the same source of truth. I worked closely with engineers to make sure design principles translated into robust implementation, and used that implementation to identify where the system itself needed to evolve.

Colour works in three layers. Primitives are the raw values and are never used directly. Theme gives each brand, in light and dark, a set of named roles, and Surface adjusts those roles for the ground a block sits on, so one component reads correctly on white, tint, brand or navy without a variant. Every pairing passes WCAG AA, and Figma and code are built only from these library tokens and components.

As the system matured, I pushed beyond individual components, turning recurring problems, such as multi-step journeys, dynamic forms and validation, into reusable patterns teams could apply instead of starting from scratch. Form validation is one example: designed once, the same error summary and messages now run on every form in all three products. It confirmed how I think about Design Systems: a pattern solved once should never need solving again. The system's job is to make reuse the easy path, not the exception.

In Figma, a master library mirrored the code, with colour and type bound to brand variables, so switching brand, theme or direction in a design file works exactly as it does in the product. Storybook and the documentation site became a shared reference point. Designers could see how components were implemented, engineers could see design intent, behaviour and expected states. This reduced ambiguity and made the system less dependent on individual people knowing how everything worked; the Design System became a shared product between the two disciplines, not something handed from one to the other.

As adoption grew, governance mattered more, but I didn't want it to become an approval process. Instead, I ran regular alignment sessions with the front-end team, so design and code moved together. Around that sat a roadmap, a contribution model and onboarding for new teams. The aim was shared ownership, not central control.

Applying the system to real products

I worked with product teams to apply the system across Member, Adviser and Employer, in regular working sessions around real customer journeys with product designers, our content designer, engineers, product owners and subject-matter experts. Every product problem we solved fed a pattern back into the system, and working on real journeys exposed gaps far faster than designing components in isolation.

As we applied the system, we also improved the products themselves, guided by research and tested as we went. We started with the original product: we mapped the member lifecycle, asked members why they signed in, and studied what they contacted support about. Most came to check their balance, contributions and investments, yet finding the latest balance took "three clicks too many". So the new dashboard leads with those, while the calculator and beneficiaries, used less often, sit further down. We then tested the new design with members of basic, intermediate and advanced pension knowledge. They described it as clear, concise and easy to use, and testing confirmed the order of the information.

Every brand, language and device

Smart needed to support different brands and white-label partners without building separate products, so I helped establish a theming approach based on shared functionality, components and code, with brand expression handled through configuration. The underlying experience stayed consistent while the visual expression changed, which became increasingly important as Smart expanded internationally. The principle: don't create a new Design System every time a new brand appears. Build the platform so brand differences can be expressed through configuration.

Multi-brand theming

One dashboard, every brand

All three apps were built once and reskinned per partner: Smart, Zurich, New Ireland and Keystone, each in light and dark. Shown here is the member dashboard. Layout and components stay fixed; only colour, type and iconography change.

Scalability also meant anticipating requirements before they became one-off problems: building support for right-to-left languages into the platform rather than treating Arabic as an isolated request, and integrating localisation into the workflow so content could evolve independently from interface design. So when Zurich needed Arabic, it shipped on the existing products as a setting, not as a separate build, in a fraction of the time a one-off would have taken. Don't solve today's exception. Improve the platform so the next team doesn't have the same problem.

Right to left

The member experience, in Arabic

Right to left is built into all three apps, for any partner that needs it. Shown here is the member app for Zurich. Layout mirrors; components and tokens don't change.

The member app also had to ship on iOS and Android. Rather than design a separate product, I treated native as one more setting of the system, next to brand, theme and direction. The components, tokens and page templates stay the same. Only the shell changes: the status bar, a native header in place of the page heading, a tab bar in place of the menu, bottom sheets for light questions, and Face ID or fingerprint at sign-in, each following its own platform's conventions.

Measuring whether it worked

The real test wasn't how many components we built. It was whether teams could deliver better products with less duplication. The US team built their own products on the system from the start and delivered roughly 50% faster than forecast, a forecast made before they knew there was a design system to build on. The saving ran across design, compliance, stakeholder review and development, and the London team saw the same speed-up. For members, advisers and employers, every product built on the system met WCAG AA, with all 1,550 colour pairings checked in Figma and code.

What I learned

The clearest product lesson came from support. People called to ask Smart to make changes for them, not because the features were missing, but because they couldn't work out how to use them. Designing for clarity, with plain language, short steps and choices explained by what happens to your money, mattered more than adding anything new.

Design Systems rarely fail because someone built the wrong button. They struggle when teams aren't aligned, ownership is unclear and the system isn't connected to real delivery. What worked was staying hands-on while creating the conditions for other teams to succeed.

What I'd do now

Since Smart, I've been exploring how AI changes the way products like this are designed and built. Here's what I'd bring to it today:

  • Prototype in code, earlier: test real journeys with realistic data in working prototypes, rather than static screens, so we learn faster.
  • Keep quality consistent at scale: use AI to check content, accessibility and pattern use as more teams build on the system.
  • Let AI build from the system, not around it: agents that reuse what exists before creating anything new, with people making the final call.
